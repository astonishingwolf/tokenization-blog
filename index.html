When did discrete representation get so bad?

Flow-based policies have emerged as the de facto parameterization of choice for robot learning, while discrete autoregressive (AR) alternatives are often dismissed on the grounds that quantizing continuous actions necessarily sacrifices fidelity. Yet, in other modalities, such as audio, autoregressive models have proven remarkably effective despite relying on discrete representations. This contrast raises a natural question: Is quantization really what holds back autoregressive policies in robotics? The answer is perhaps surprising: quantization error alone is often insufficient to explain the failure of discrete policies. In the majority of cases, policies fail by a wide margin and cannot be explained by quantization error alone. Moreover, discrete actions are in fact more precise in tail-end situations. So why do discrete policies fail in the first place? As a starting point, we argue that the primary limitations of autoregressive action generation are uneven information allocation and error accumulation, rather than quantization error. In commonly used action representations such as FAST, uneven information allocation leads to variation in conditional token entropy across positions, which may result in unstable training dynamics. On the other hand, for action binning, long autoregressive sequences lead to error accumulation and degraded performance. To address both problems, we advocate a simple learned tokenization that encourages even information allocation while maintaining compression.

Why did FAST and Action Binning fail ?

In the world of LLMs, tokenization is often seen as a backward form of learning that is redundant. However, the issue is that language is already one of the most compressed forms of information representation. A single word can pack a substantial amount of information, so the entire representation is already quite compressed to begin with.

However, actions are different. A robot operating at 30 Hz can very well be represented at 15 Hz with some small changes. Then the question becomes: Why is compression necessary?

The idea is that the more compressed the esentation becomes, the easier the information assignment becomes. In fact, compression leads to shorter generation horizons, which in turn reduce error accumulation and ultimately lead to better policies.

Okay, but FAST already does compression. Why did it fail? Fair question. The problem with FAST is its uneven information allocation. Splitting the action representation into different DCT coefficients leads to the first few tokens having extremely high entropy, while the suffix tokens essentially encode noise. This leads to something much worse: due to exposure bias, an early token error during autoregressive generation can degrade subsequent predictions.

Background on Tokenization.

Tokenization is a simple way of mapping continuous representations to discrete units. But why do we want discrete units in the first place? Autoregressive models, such as GPT, are commonly trained to predict discrete tokens using a cross-entropy loss. This makes discrete representations a natural choice for autoregressive action generation.

Traditionally, vector quantization (VQ) [vq] has been used to obtain discrete representations. VQ maps continuous latents to entries in a learned codebook. However, recent approaches such as finite scalar quantization (FSQ) [fsq] provide a simpler alternative. Rather than learning a codebook, FSQ independently quantizes each latent dimension into a finite set of values.

This induces a grid-like structure in the latent space, providing a natural notion of distance and neighborhood between discrete representations. We argue that this structure provides a useful inductive bias for autoregressive action generation.

Overall, the quantization scheme is simple and effective.

Architecture of the Tokenizer

It is generally accepted that the reconstruction quality of a tokenizer is not necessarily indicative of the success of the downstream generative model. We find that it is equally important to distribute information appropriately across the token space. This motivates our tokenizer architecture, where compression and information allocation are the primary objectives, rather than the sharpness of the reconstruction. In particular, we argue that less smooth motions with higher jerk provide a worse inductive bias for autoregressive policies.

A 1D tokenizer can be viewed as a variant of tokenization in which the latent tokens need not correspond to individual timesteps or follow a fixed temporal ordering. We therefore consider a simple architecture that allows us to represent an action sequence using an unordered set of latent tokens. Our architecture is inspired by the recent work TiTok [titok].

Specifically, we introduce a set of learnable embeddings, which are concatenated with the action embeddings and processed to obtain latent representations. We then apply finite scalar quantization (FSQ) to these latents to obtain discrete tokens.

The discrete latents are subsequently passed through a cross-attention module with another set of learnable embeddings. The resulting representations are then processed by a Transformer decoder to reconstruct the original action sequence.
